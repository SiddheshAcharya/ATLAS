## What the field agrees on

The field generally agrees that gut microbiome diversity is strongly linked to mood-related behaviors, including major depressive disorder [doi:10.1515/revneuro-2017-0072, doi:10.12775/jehs.2025.77.55276]. Alterations in gut microbial composition, such as reduced diversity and dysbiosis, are consistently associated with a higher prevalence and severity of depressive symptoms [doi:10.1515/revneuro-2017-0072, doi:10.12775/jehs.2025.77.55276]. These changes are understood to play a role in depression's pathophysiology through immune, neuroendocrine, and neurotransmitter pathways [doi:10.1515/revneuro-2017-0072, doi:10.12775/jehs.2025.77.55276]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies targeting the gut microbiota, including probiotics, prebiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, and dietary interventions like the Mediterranean diet, are recognized for their potential to alleviate depressive symptoms and improve mental health outcomes [doi:10.1515/revneuro-2017-0072, doi:10.12775/jehs.2025.77.55276].

## Where the field disagrees

A contradiction exists regarding the consistency of findings on the gut microbiome-depression association. One paper asserts that gut dysbiosis has been repeatedly observed in connection with depression [doi:10.31435/ijitss.2(50).2026.5956]. Conversely, another study indicates that clinical investigations into the gut microbiome-depression link have yielded mixed results [doi:10.1038/s41522-025-00707-9]. This contradiction has not been resolved within the provided literature.
