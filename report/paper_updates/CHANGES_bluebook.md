### 2.3 — design decisions as built
**Old:** Two design decisions distinguish this proposal from the systems reviewed in Section 2.1 and 2.2. First, the coordinator that drives the four phases is ordinary, testable code, and the language model is invoked only as a bounded tool at three specific points — extraction, relationship judgement and final synthesis — rather than being placed in charge of the control loop itself, which keeps the system's behaviour reproducible and its cost attributable. Second, orchestration is split across two tiers: a durable, crash-resumable macro loop governs the overall run, since a complete pass can occupy on the order of thirty minutes and a naive process that fails partway through would discard everything read up to that point, while a lighter, second-scale controller handles the inner reasoning steps of tool selection and retry within a single iteration. The remainder of this report develops the requirement analysis, design and implementation of this proposal in detail.

**New:** Two design decisions distinguish this proposal from the systems reviewed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2. First, the coordinator that drives the four phases is ordinary, testable code, and the language model is invoked only as a bounded tool at fixed points — keyword expansion, claim extraction, relationship judgement, synthesis and chat — rather than being placed in charge of the control loop itself, which keeps the system's behaviour reproducible and its cost attributable. Second, every model call and every API response is cached on its exact input, so a run that fails partway — or is simply run again — replays completed work from the cache at no model cost and produces identical output; this gives the resumability a durable workflow engine was originally planned to provide, without running one. The remainder of this report develops the requirement analysis, design and implementation of this proposal in detail.

**New:** Each candidate paper d in the frontier F is scored from five signals computed from metadata and abstracts only, so that scoring the entire frontier remains inexpensive relative to the cost of reading any single paper in full. ### 4.2.3 — pairing and judge as built
**Old:** The number of unordered claim pairs over ten thousand stored claims is on the order of fifty million, which forbids adjudicating every pair individually with a model. Figure 10 sets out the two-stage reduction. Stage A is purely arithmetic: a pair survives only if both claims fall in the same Leiden community, their embeddings exceed a similarity of 0.72, they share at least one scope-field value, and a lexical polarity screen indicates opposing direction — four conditions removing approximately 99.994 per cent of candidate pairs at negligible cost. Stage B is expensive and rare: a model reads both claims with the methods sections they were drawn from and returns one of five verdicts — SUPPORTS, REPLICATES, CONTRADICTS, UNRELATED or DIFFERENT_SCOPE — the last justifying scope fields at all, since two papers reporting opposite findings at different experimental scales have not contradicted one another, and the correct output is an observation about the moderating variable rather than a flagged disagreement.

**New:** The number of unordered claim pairs over ten thousand stored claims is on the order of fifty million, which forbids judging every pair with a model. Candidate generation is therefore arithmetic: for each new claim, the ten nearest stored claims from other papers with cosine similarity of at least 0.55 are retrieved, capped at twelve pairs per paper and 400 judge calls per run, and only these pairs reach the model — on the evaluated runs, 93.7–99.1 % of all possible pairs were never judged (Figure 10). The judge then reads both claims with their quotations and scope records and returns one of six verdicts — SUPPORTS, EXTENDS, REPLICATES, CONTRADICTS, UNRELATED or DIFFERENT_SCOPE — with a confidence and a rationale. DIFFERENT_SCOPE is what justifies the scope fields: two papers reporting opposite findings at different experimental scales have not contradicted one another, and the correct output is an observation about the moderating variable rather than a flagged disagreement.

### 4.2.4 — fusion as built
**Old:** The six normalised outputs are fused into GapScore(g) = alpha.Novelty(g) + beta.Plausibility(g) + gamma.Impact(g) + delta.Feasibility(g), where Novelty is structural distance between the gap's endpoints, Plausibility is the strength of the bridging evidence, Impact combines concept centrality with citation velocity, and Feasibility asks whether a method already exists to test the gap. A hard rule applies before anything is written to the final report: a gap that cannot name the specific papers bounding it is discarded, with no confidence threshold to relax this — the entire practical difference between this phase and asking a general-purpose model what the gaps in a field are.

**New:** Each detector's scores are min-max normalised within that detector, near-duplicate gaps are merged (keeping every detector that found them), and the lists are fused by weighted reciprocal-rank fusion — each detector contributes w_d / (60 + rank of the gap in that detector) — with a bonus of 0.15 for gaps found independently by more than one detector. A hard rule applies before anything is written to the final report: a gap that cannot name the specific papers bounding it is discarded, with no confidence threshold to relax this — the entire practical difference between this phase and asking a general-purpose model what the gaps in a field are.

### 5.2 — relations
**New:** Relation adjudication. The accuracy of the judge could not be established, because the reference labels were themselves unreliable. On 12 claim pairs a human annotator labelled with a single verdict, the human and an AI annotator agreed on 2 (κ = −0.17), the human and the judge on 2 (κ = −0.05), and the AI annotator and the judge on 5 (κ = 0.22); merging the labels into three classes did not rescue agreement (κ = −0.07).
