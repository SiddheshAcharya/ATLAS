### Abstract — design + measured results
**Old:** The volume of peer-reviewed output now exceeds what any individual can read, and the tools built to cope with that volume are almost exclusively retrieval tools: they rank documents and hand the reading, the reconciliation of disagreements, and the identification of unasked questions back to the human. This paper presents ATLAS (Agentic Technology for Literature Analysis and Synthesis), an autonomous literature-synthesis agent that treats reading as a resource-allocation problem rather than a ranking problem. ATLAS seeds a corpus from four open scholarly APIs, then enters a foraging loop in which exactly one paper is read in full per iteration; the paper is chosen by a seven-signal priority function whose Novelty and Contest terms are computed from what the agent has already learned, so that the act of reading changes what is read next. Every claim the system extracts carries a verbatim quotation and a character interval into the parsed source, and a deterministic verifier discards any claim whose interval does not reproduce the quotation, which converts hallucination from a quality that must be argued about into a pass rate that can be reported. Claim pairs are adjudicated in two stages, an arithmetic screen that removes 99.99 % of candidate pairs followed by an adjudicator that reads both methods sections and can return DIFFERENT_SCOPE rather than a spurious contradiction. The resulting knowledge graph is then examined by six structurally independent gap detectors, fused into a single ranked list under a hard rule that no gap may be reported unless it names the papers that bound it. A measured v0 build exposed four concrete failure modes that shaped this architecture, including an edge density of 65.9 % where real citation networks exhibit 1-5 %, and a dataset difference labelled CONTRADICTS at confidence 0.90. The system is reported at 24.9 % weighted implementation against an eleven-subsystem denominator defined in advance, together with the evaluation protocol, baselines and target metrics against which the remaining work will be judged.

**New:** The volume of peer-reviewed output now exceeds what any individual can read, and the tools built to cope with that volume are almost exclusively retrieval tools: they rank documents and hand the reading, the reconciliation of disagreements, and the identification of unasked questions back to the human. This paper presents ATLAS (Agentic Technology for Literature Analysis and Synthesis), an autonomous literature-synthesis agent that treats reading as a resource-allocation problem rather than a ranking problem. ATLAS seeds a corpus from four open scholarly APIs, then enters a foraging loop in which one paper is read per iteration; the paper is chosen by a weighted priority function whose Novelty term is computed from what the agent has already read, so that the act of reading changes what is read next. Every claim the system extracts carries a verbatim quotation that a deterministic verifier must find in the parsed source, and any claim whose quotation cannot be found is discarded, which converts hallucination from a quality that must be argued about into a pass rate that can be reported. Candidate claim pairs are generated by nearest-neighbour search before a language-model judge that may return DIFFERENT_SCOPE rather than a spurious contradiction, and the resulting knowledge graph is examined by gap detectors fused under a hard rule that no gap may be reported unless it names the papers that bound it. The completed system was evaluated end to end on three research questions. 49 of 50 audited quotations were verbatim in their sources (98 %, against a 95 % target); a replay of a 15-paper run reproduced its reading order, 65 claims and 132 relations exactly with no new model calls; and 93.7–99.1 % of candidate claim pairs were excluded before any model was consulted. The foraging loop read a substantially different set of papers from similarity-ranked retrieval (36–56 % overlap) and reached 4–60 % more of the surrounding citation network, though not more citation clusters at a 15-paper budget. Relation adjudication and gap detection are the weakest components: annotators themselves disagreed on relation labels (κ = −0.17) and most detected gaps were artefacts of wording, and both outcomes are reported.

### 3.3.3 — verification as built
**Old:** The verification step is the point of the design and is deliberately unintelligent. The stored text is sliced at the declared interval, both strings are normalised for whitespace and ligature variation introduced by PDF extraction, and the two are compared. On a mismatch the extraction is retried once; on a second failure the claim is deleted and a rejection event is written to the log. Nothing unverified is stored, so the graph cannot be polluted by fabricated evidence, and the proportion of proposed claims that survive becomes a reportable quantity, the grounding pass rate. This is what allows a small local model to be used for the bulk extraction work: a weaker model produces fewer claims, not false ones, and the degradation is visible in a metric rather than latent in the output.

**New:** The verification step is the point of the design and is deliberately unintelligent. The quotation is searched for in the normalised source text, and an exact match is stored with its character offsets. Otherwise a tolerant match is allowed only if the quotation matches exactly once whitespace, hyphenation, quotation marks, letter case and bracketed reference markers are ignored; an earlier 97 %-similarity fallback was removed after an audit showed it admitting real edits such as a dropped word. A claim that fails is dropped and a rejection is written to the log; it is not retried or repaired. Nothing unverified is stored, and the proportion of proposed claims that survive becomes a reportable quantity. ### 3.4 — D3 as built
**Old:** Detector D3 warrants illustration because it is the least familiar. The corpus is projected onto a sparse tensor whose axes are method family, operating scale, task family and year, and each cell counts the studies occupying that combination. A gap is not simply an empty cell, since most cells in a large tensor are empty and most of those emptinesses are uninteresting. A gap is an empty cell whose neighbourhood is densely populated, which is the configuration under which the absence is surprising.

**New:** Detector D3 warrants illustration because it is the least familiar. Methods and datasets extracted from the corpus form the rows and columns of a matrix, and each cell counts the studies that applied that method to that dataset. Only methods and datasets reported by at least three papers are kept, since most cells of a sparse matrix are empty and most of those emptinesses are uninteresting; an empty cell between a well-used method and a well-used dataset is the configuration under which the absence is surprising. The design's four-axis tensor with non-negative factorisation is simplified to this two-axis form in the present build.

**New:** Each detector's scores are min-max normalised within that detector; near-duplicate gaps (statement similarity at least 0.85, or bounding-paper overlap at least 0.6) are merged, keeping every detector that found them; and the lists are fused by weighted reciprocal-rank fusion,

### 3.4 — fusion formula
**Old:** GapScore(g) = α·Novelty(g) + β·Plausibility(g) + γ·Impact(g) + δ·Feasibility(g)

**New:** GapScore(g) = Σ_d w_d / (60 + rank_d(g)) + corroboration bonus

### 3.4 — fusion explanation
**Old:** where Novelty is structural distance in the graph between the endpoints of the gap, Plausibility is the strength of the bridging evidence connecting them, Impact combines concept centrality with citation velocity in the surrounding neighbourhood, and Feasibility asks whether a method already exists that could test the gap. A hard rule is then applied before anything is written: a gap that cannot name the specific papers bounding it is discarded. This is not a confidence threshold and cannot be relaxed by an operator, and it is the entire practical difference between this phase and asking a language model what the gaps in a field are.

**New:** where rank_d(g) is the rank of g within detector d, the detector weights w_d favour the more reliable signals (for example 1.2 for unresolved conflicts), and a bonus of 0.15 rewards gaps found independently by more than one detector. The design's four-factor score of novelty, plausibility, impact and feasibility is replaced by this rank fusion, which needs no calibration across detectors. ### 3.6 — orchestration as built
**Old:** Orchestration is split across two tiers for a reason that is empirical rather than aesthetic. An Extra Deep run occupies roughly thirty minutes of wall-clock time, and a plain loop that fails at paper 240 loses everything preceding it. A durable execution engine writes each completed step to a log, so a replacement worker replays the log and resumes at the next paper. The inner reasoning steps, which last seconds and involve tool selection and retry, are handled by a lighter graph-structured controller. This division is defensible on the grounds that the two loops have different failure characteristics and different time constants, and that resumability was already on the requirements list before it was recognised as durable execution by name.

**New:** Orchestration is a plain asynchronous Python loop, and every stage can be run on its own from the command line, which is how the system is debugged without repeating a multi-hour crawl. The evaluated build obtains the same property more cheaply: every language-model call is cached on its job, model, parameters, prompt version and input, and every API response is cached on disk, so re-running a crashed or finished run replays completed work from the cache at no model cost. ### 4.1 — instruments actually used

### 4.6 — measured gap result
**Old:** Gap output is the hardest of the four instruments to evaluate, because there is no ground-truth register of unasked questions. Two procedures are therefore adopted. ### 4.9 — limitations (1)
**Old:** Several limitations are inherent to the approach rather than incidental to its current state. Coverage is bounded by open access: roughly forty-five per cent of reachable works have a legally retrievable full text, and the remainder are read at abstract level, which weakens scope adjudication precisely where it is most needed. ### 4.9 — limitations (2)
**Old:** Third, the adjudicator is a language model and remains the least reliable component in the system. The grounding gate bounds the damage on the extraction side, since an unverifiable claim is deleted, but no equivalent mechanical check exists for a relation judgement: a wrong verdict on a real pair of real claims is not detectable by string comparison. The two-stage design reduces exposure by ensuring the model is consulted rarely and only on pairs that have already passed four independent filters, and confidence values with written rationales are stored so that a disputed edge can be inspected, but this is mitigation rather than proof. Fourth, the gain-decay stopping rule can in principle halt during a genuine plateau that precedes a productive region of the graph; the trailing window is set conservatively at twelve iterations for this reason.
